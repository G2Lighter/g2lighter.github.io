Summary · Part 1

The Case of H.M.

Born 1926 in Connecticut. Severe epilepsy from age 10. In 1953, an experimental surgery removed both hippocampi . Seizures improved — but he could no longer form new declarative memories .

Summary · Part 2

Permanent present tense

Working memory lasted only 30–60 seconds . Every person was a stranger. Every day was the first day. He lived entirely in the present — with no past to hold onto and no future to imagine.

Summary · Part 3

Memory is not one system

Declarative memory (facts, events) depends on the hippocampus. Procedural memory (skills, habits) does not. In the mirror star-tracing task, H.M. improved daily — yet never remembered practising.

Summary · Part 4

A legacy in neuroscience

Called "the most important patient ever studied in neuropsychology." After his death in 2008, his brain was sliced into 2,401 sections and digitized — an enduring memorial to the man who forgot everything.

Response · Personal

Memory in the digital age

The article notes that we increasingly store memory outside our bodies: notes, phones, the cloud. I do this too — calendars, photos, chat logs. But H.M. had no choice: his declarative memory lived entirely outside him .

"After the operation, all of Molaison's declarative memory lived outside his own body, while only some of ours does."

This made me ask: if we outsource memory, do we lose part of ourselves? Or does memory simply become something we share — with people, with devices, with the world?

Because in the end, we are not only what we remember — we are also what we choose to notice, to keep, and to share.
