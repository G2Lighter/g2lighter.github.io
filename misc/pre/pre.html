Summary · Part 1

A Life Interrupted

Epilepsy began at ten and worsened through his teens, bringing seizures severe enough to disrupt school, work, and any chance at an ordinary social life.

Summary · Part 3

The Cost of Relief

The surgery quieted the seizures, but removing both hippocampi and much of the medial temporal lobe severed his ability to form new declarative memories.

Summary · Part 2

The Experimental Choice

By the early 1950s, with drugs failing and seizures unrelenting, his doctors proposed an experimental brain operation — one that had never been tried in this precise form.

Summary · Part 4

From Henry to H.M.

Researchers stripped away his name, publishing him only as "H.M." — and the man who couldn't remember himself became the most famous patient in neuroscience.

Response · The Way We Remember

Memory in the digital age

The article notes that we increasingly store memory outside our bodies: notes, phones, the cloud.

"After the operation, all of Molaison's declarative memory lived outside his own body, while only some of ours does."

But the medium only stores; it doesn't remember. If we don't actively look back, our past is just dead data.

Response · A Call to Remember

H.M. could never remember —
but we can.

Every photo we keep, every note we write, every story we tell— they are just data until we give them meaning. We have the power to turn the past into memory.

So maybe the real lesson of his life is not what he lost — it is what we so often take for granted.
